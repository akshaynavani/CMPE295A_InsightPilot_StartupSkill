InsightPilot · Mission, vision and values

A value phrased as a virtue costs nothing. Each of these names what is given up

Mission · true today

We make an analytical answer something you can check in seconds instead of something you have to trust.

Not democratising data: the analyst is not being democratised, they are being given back the hour verification costs. Not AI for analytics: the AI is a means and the market is full of it. The mission names the thing that is actually different, which is the cost of checking.
Vision · ten years out

An analytical answer that arrives without its trace looks the way an unaudited financial statement looks now: not wrong, but not usable for anything that matters.

A claim about a norm rather than market share. The strongest version of success is that the practice outlives the product, and that buyers start asking every vendor for a verification cost ratio, including vendors that are not us.
Value one
Publish the number that makes us look worse
The trade-off

We lose the impressive headline to keep the credible one

Already decided

The mechanism arithmetic came to roughly 2.9 times, not ten, and was published with its band and its conservative corner. A 13.8-times queueing effect was computed and withdrawn, because induced demand consumes it. On a single-source familiar question the gain is about 1.0 or below and the analyst should keep writing SQL

What it costs

Every pitch is less exciting than the one a competitor can give

Why we accept it

The graveyard in this category was filled by overpromising. One documented failure was overpromise against delivery, and another shipped inside the most widely deployed BI tool on earth and still retired. In a market disappointed four times, the scarce asset is a claim that survives being checked

Value two
Refuse the question rather than guess at it
The trade-off

We are slower and more annoying than a system that just answers

Already decided

When a term resolves to more than one column or grain, the system halts and asks instead of taking the best guess. On a first encounter with a source this makes us measurably slower, about 0.85 times, before it ever pays back

What it costs

A worse demo, and a first session that feels like an interrogation

Why we accept it

Schema-level errors are 81.2% of analysed text-to-SQL failures, and failure looks like a plausible but incorrect answer. A silent guess samples directly from the dominant failure distribution, and the user has no way to detect it

Value three
Verify with different code, never with the model that produced the answer
The trade-off

We decline the most fashionable techniques in our own field

Already decided

Reflection, self-critique, model-as-judge and multi-agent debate are declined on one principle: they keep verification inside the thing being verified. Our checks are plain SQL: row counts, grain assertions, coverage, boundary totals

What it costs

We look less sophisticated to an audience expecting to hear those words

Why we accept it

A model asked to check its own output shares the failure that produced it, and the strongest incumbent reached the same conclusion in production. The side benefit is economic: verification consumes zero model tokens, so the differentiating mechanism is the cheapest thing in the system

Value four
The person who cannot verify does not get to ship unverified
The trade-off

We renounce the largest revenue segment in our category

Already decided

A business stakeholder can ask anything and gets an answer in ninety seconds. They cannot export it without an analyst's sign-off. We are not building self-service for people who cannot judge correctness

What it costs

The whole non-technical market, where the category's revenue and attention are

Why we accept it

The purest test of the opposite bet cleared a 73.67% markdown against its last primary round. The simpler version: an answer someone cannot check, put in front of their boss, is a person set up to be wrong in public. Gating it is not paternalism, because the alternative is that they guess, which 76% already do

Value five
Consume what the customer already built; do not replace it
The trade-off

We depend on artifacts we do not control

Already decided

Where a semantic layer exists, its definitions take precedence outright over anything we infer. We will not become the place metric definitions live

What it costs

Deeper dependence on tooling maintained for another product, and a smaller surface to sell

Why we accept it

59% of large enterprises already fund semantic layers. Telling a buyer that spend is unnecessary loses the deal and is false: a layer says what a metric means, not what happened or why. Requiring one would inherit exactly the bounded-scope failure we are built against

Why we exist

The industry answered the wrong question

Asked to make enterprise data accessible, every serious vendor built something that answers faster. The measured evidence says speed was not the binding constraint: trust in data went from 66% to 83% in a year, 71% fear wrong output reaching stakeholders, and the money is moving toward constraining AI output. Meanwhile about 25% of employees use the BI tools their employer bought, flat across seven years. A faster answer nobody will stake their name on is a thing you have to go and check, which is the work you were avoiding.

What would make us abandon this

A mission that cannot be falsified is a slogan

If verification costs as much as re-derivation, the mission is unachievable and we should say so publicly rather than continue. The beachhead's objection is correct as stated and untested, and one afternoon with five analysts settles it. Publishing that negative result would be more consistent with the first value than quietly pivoting to a faster chat box, and it would still be a good capstone, because the field has no public measurement either way.
