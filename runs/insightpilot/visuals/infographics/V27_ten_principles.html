A feature that maps to none of these gets cut, or the list was wrong

Each of these is an empirical finding from the research layer, not a design preference. That is what makes the rule enforceable: a feature can be checked against evidence rather than argued about. The two shaded rows are the ones that carry the most weight in the design.

P1Schema grounding dominates language understanding 81.2% of 4,602 analysed failures are wrong column or misread meaning Spend engineering on grounding and disambiguation, not conversational polish
P2Error compounds multiplicatively across steps Capability and reliability trade against each other; propagation is the primary agent bottleneck Keep plans short. Verify per step. Never add a step for elegance
P3Failure is plausible, not obvious "Failure looks like a plausible but incorrect answer" Surface doubt, not confidence. No confidence scores presented as accuracy

P5Verification cost is the adoption gate, not accuracy 71% fear hallucinated output reaching stakeholders; 24.9% name accuracy as their top reservation Design for cheap checking rather than for autonomy. Optimise the verify beat

What P2, P5 and P10 say together

The design follows from an admission

This product cannot promise reliable autonomous answers, because the field cannot deliver them. It can promise that when the system is wrong, the analyst finds out in seconds rather than after the number reaches a slide. That is the whole design, and it is derived rather than chosen.

The principle with no feature

P10 is satisfied by a practice, not a build

Nine principles carry at least one flagship feature. P10 carries none, because the answer to it is an ordering decision: build the harness and the fixed question set before the agents. Stated plainly in the coverage table rather than filled with an invented feature.
